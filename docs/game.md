## The player's role

You control the nation's **executive leadership: the government and its cabinet**, rather than one named politician. You propose reforms and budgets, administer authorized programs, negotiate within your powers, and respond to events. Your authority is limited by the nation's laws, administrative capacity, political support, and monetary arrangements.

Lawmakers, courts, independent monetary institutions, households, and businesses act within their own roles. Greater state direction can expand your responsibilities, but also requires information and implementation capacity. The executive role and its relationship to other institutions must fit each nation's historical profile.

The preferred working proposal is to continue playing across successive governments. A leadership change alters your mandate and coalition while preserving the economy and its obligations. Exact election and succession rules remain open. See [The executive, lawmakers & courts]({{ '/government/' | relative_url }}) for authority, bargaining, legal review, and campaign continuity.

## A yearly turn

1. **Read the report.** See what changed, who benefited, which earlier decisions are taking effect, and what is becoming a problem.
2. **Choose priorities.** Proposed limit: two major initiatives, alongside adjustments to recurring budget priorities. Continuing projects keep using funds and administrative capacity. Major changes should not bypass the initiative limit merely by being labeled a budget adjustment.
3. **Negotiate and respond.** Seek legislative support and consider amendments; address major legal challenges, corporate proposals, bank requests, or trade negotiations. An energy shock, strike, innovation, or harvest failure may require a response. Major new commitments compete for the same resources as planned initiatives.
4. **Advance the year.** Actors respond, projects progress, and institutions implement their decisions. The central bank and regulators act under their mandates; firms and households respond to the resulting conditions. The report explains results and unfinished effects.

The initiative limit is a proposed pacing rule. Funding, real resources, implementation capacity, and legal authority are separate constraints. A policy can be affordable on paper yet impossible to deliver this year.

An annual turn summarizes developments within the year; it does not imply that real actors wait a year between every response. The ordering and handling of urgent events remain design decisions. The player should see likely institutional responses before committing, without receiving a guaranteed outcome.

## What the player controls

| Relationship | Typical decisions | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Executive administration | Implement authorized programs and use delegated powers | Funding, scope of authority, administrative capacity, and delivery time |
| Legislative initiative | Propose budgets, taxes, and reforms that need legislation | Relevant chambers, voting thresholds, amendments, and legal limits |
| Negotiation | Corporate investment conditions, trade concessions, or transition arrangements | The counterpart can reject, counteroffer, or fail to deliver |
| Institutional influence | Appointments or mandate reforms where lawful | Independence, legal process, shared authority, and political support; no automatic control over rulings or independent rate decisions |
| Economic influence | Change incentives facing lenders, producers, workers, and consumers | Actors decide within their own resources and obligations |
| External exposure | Prepare for world demand, commodity shocks, or foreign policy | The player cannot simply set external conditions |

This distinction should remain visible as the player steers toward more planning or more market coordination. Gaining direct allocation powers also creates information, monitoring, and implementation duties.

## Policy choices

| Dimension | Examples of decisions |
| --- | --- |
| Ownership | Nationalize electricity, privatize an enterprise, support cooperatives, change public-enterprise governance |
| Allocation | Rely on market prices, set strategic investment priorities, introduce binding production targets |
| Competition | Simplify business entry, challenge monopolies, regulate a network utility |
| Public provision | Expand public healthcare, subsidize childcare, change eligibility for a guaranteed service |
| Redistribution | Adjust taxes, transfers, unemployment insurance, or pensions |
| Regulation | Set labor, environmental, financial, and consumer rules; fund enforcement and review outcomes |
| Trade and investment | Change tariffs, support an industry, permit foreign investment, diversify suppliers |
| Institutions | Reform procurement, strengthen administration, or change a monetary institution where legally possible |

Policies should be concrete packages with a cost, expected mechanism, implementation time, and known uncertainties. National institutional constraints can make some choices unavailable or require preliminary reforms.

## What a policy proposal contains

Before committing, the player should see the objective, affected sectors and groups, legal route, funding source, recurring obligations, required workers or inputs, expected timing, and plausible NPC responses. Estimates should distinguish established relationships from uncertain forecasts.

Conditional support needs milestones, monitoring, and an expiry or review date. Public guarantees must disclose contingent liabilities as well as current expenditure. Ownership changes must account for assets, obligations, governance, and management.

After adoption, proposals become ongoing commitments with states such as authorized, preparing, underway, delayed, completed, or under review. Cancellation can stop future commitments where allowed but does not recover all previous spending or undo completed changes.

## Example: an electricity crisis

Electricity becomes expensive and supply is unreliable. Households need relief; industry faces disruptions. You could:

- Subsidize household bills, targeting support or applying it broadly.
- Cap prices and decide whether and how producers are compensated.
- Encourage private investment through contracts or regulatory reform.
- Fund public generation and grid capacity.
- Invest in efficiency and reduced demand.

Each option addresses a different part of the problem. Immediate support uses fiscal capacity. New infrastructure requires labor, equipment, and time. A price cap's effect depends on its level, supply conditions, enforcement, and compensation.

The scenario should ask the player to combine measures and manage the transition. It should not embed one policy as the universally correct answer.

See the [four-turn electricity walkthrough]({{ '/scenarios/' | relative_url }}#electricity-reliability-and-ownership) for an example involving a public network operator, private generators, regulation, and household support.

## Outcomes and success

The player chooses a mandate, such as reducing poverty, building industrial capacity, or expanding universal services. The end-of-campaign report evaluates that mandate alongside its costs and wider consequences.

Track a limited dashboard, with detail available on demand:

- Living standards and access to essentials.
- Employment and productivity.
- Inequality and economic insecurity.
- Inflation and shortages.
- Public finances and debt-service pressures.
- Environmental conditions and resilience.

Reports should show how outcomes differ across households, regions, and businesses. A national average can improve while a particular group becomes worse off.

Avoid a universal score that quietly defines one economic ideology as victory. Mandates can have measurable targets, while the final assessment remains multidimensional.

## The end-of-turn report

The first view should identify the most important changes, the status of major commitments, and emerging decisions. Deeper explanations then connect outcomes to policies, actor choices, constraints, and shocks.

For example, report that housing approvals increased while completions lagged, explain a construction bottleneck, show which bank changed lending, and identify who still cannot obtain housing. Avoid treating announced spending as completed service delivery.

Show observations separately from estimates. Corporate promises are not confirmed future output, a regulator's preliminary finding is not a final ruling, and a forecast is not a guaranteed next-turn result.

Report institutional outcomes separately: what lawmakers authorized, whether a court paused or invalidated a measure, and what the central bank decided. Show actual borrowing conditions alongside the policy rate, including which debts will reprice soon. Distinguish the economic effects of a legal delay from the effects of changing finance costs.

## Crises and recovery

Banking distress, fiscal stress, shortages, and loss of political support should constrain future choices and create recovery problems. The preferred continuity model allows leadership changes without ending play. Whether severe economic crises can end a campaign remains open; continuing through a difficult recovery may teach more than an immediate game-over screen.
