## Who the player is

The player controls the nation's **executive leadership: the government and its cabinet**, rather than one named politician or every institution in the country. The relevant executive office, powers, and relationships depend on the nation's institutions at the scenario's start.

You set the economic agenda, propose budgets and legislation, administer authorized programs, negotiate within your powers, and respond to crises. You cannot automatically command lawmakers, judges, an independent central bank, private firms, or foreign governments.

This role is the design direction. The precise treatment of leadership changes and electoral consequences remains a working proposal described below. Country-specific powers still require historical research.

## Executive powers and limits

| Executive action | What the player can attempt | What may constrain it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Set the agenda | Propose taxes, spending, ownership reforms, and economic priorities | Legislative support, legal authority, coalition commitments, and public finances |
| Administer programs | Direct ministries and implement existing legislation | Appropriated funds, statutory duties, expertise, procurement, and capacity |
| Use delegated powers | Issue regulations or executive measures within existing authority | The scope of delegation, required procedure, review, and expiry |
| Negotiate | Seek corporate investment, trade agreements, or labor-transition arrangements | Counterparty acceptance, ratification requirements, and shared authority |
| Make appointments | Fill offices where the executive has an appointment role | Eligibility, terms, confirmation, institutional independence, and removal protections |
| Reform institutions | Propose changes to mandates, powers, or governance | The applicable legislative or constitutional process and political support |

The policy briefing should state which route applies. “Propose,” “negotiate,” and “enact” are distinct actions. An executive measure must not become a universal way to bypass an unfavorable vote.

Routine administration should remain quick. Surface an institutional encounter when a major reform, disputed authority, significant commitment, or unexpected conflict makes it relevant.

## Lawmakers and political blocs

Represent legislative politics through a small number of meaningful blocs with seats or influence, constituencies, priorities, and positions on particular proposals. The structure must fit the selected nation: do not impose the same chambers, party competition, or executive-legislative relationship everywhere.

Blocs need more than one ideological coordinate. A regional bloc might favor factory support while opposing the tax used to finance it. A labor-oriented bloc might support public ownership but demand worker protections during restructuring. Government supporters may disagree among themselves.

Before a major vote, show likely support, the applicable threshold, pivotal participants, and the reasons for opposition. Estimates can be uncertain, but outcomes should arise from rules and actor commitments rather than an unexplained random veto.

Where another chamber or regional body has a necessary role, show that stage explicitly. The player should know whether support in one body is enough.

## Bargaining over a bill

A proposal can be accepted, amended, delayed, rejected, or withdrawn. Counteroffers can change eligibility, regional allocations, oversight, transition periods, funding, or expiry dates. Those terms should modify the actual policy and budget.

For example, lawmakers may support a factory subsidy only if it includes training, regional investment, reporting, and a sunset clause. The player can accept the conditions, propose alternatives, narrow the bill, or postpone it. A compromise must carry its promised obligations into future turns.

Negotiating leverage comes from explicit relationships: coalition commitments, constituency benefits, policy priorities, prior delivery, and public pressure. Avoid a single spendable “political capital” balance that purchases any law regardless of institutional rules.

The public mandate matters, but popularity alone does not authorize expenditure or alter voting thresholds. A popular bill can encounter legal or procedural limits; an unpopular measure may still pass through the appropriate process.

## Budgets, authorization, and continuity

Separate permission to establish a program from the funds available to operate it where the nation's framework makes that distinction. Existing obligations and ongoing services continue according to applicable rules while a new proposal is considered.

A rejected budget should trigger the nation's researched fallback procedure, not the same automatic shutdown in every scenario. The initial game can summarize that procedure rather than simulate every parliamentary motion.

Already funded projects continue consuming workers, inputs, and oversight. The player must distinguish a legislative announcement, authorized funding, actual expenditure, and delivered services.

## The judiciary and legal disputes

Courts are institutional actors whose review powers, independence, procedures, and remedies depend on the national profile. Their proposed role is to assess relevant questions of authority, process, rights, and obligations. They are not another popularity meter or a bloc whose votes the player can purchase.

A company, worker organization, affected household, or regional authority may bring a challenge where the legal system permits it. Not every complainant can bring every claim. The case briefing should identify the challenger, the legal issue, the court's powers, and what is at stake.

Possible outcomes, when available in that system, include upholding a measure, pausing implementation, requiring a procedural correction or compensation, or invalidating part of a decision. An interim pause is not a final judgment. Courts should not review every policy automatically; some systems also have specific required reviews that the profile must identify.

Legal uncertainty belongs in the briefing, but a ruling needs an explanation tied to the scenario's legal framework. Economic inconvenience or disagreement with the government's ideology alone should not produce a judicial veto.

## Responding to a ruling

The executive can comply, revise a measure, complete a missing procedure, seek new legislation, negotiate a settlement, or appeal where available. Each route has costs and timing. Winning a legislative vote does not automatically resolve a constitutional or rights-based objection.

For example, a court could pause a land-acquisition process because the required procedure was not followed. The player might correct the process, use a different site, or pursue an available appeal. The economic report should attribute the resulting delay to that dispute rather than to “low government efficiency.”

Appointments and institutional reform must follow the applicable rules. They should not instantly make a current court compliant or erase a pending case. Any weakening of oversight considered in a later design would require explicit institutional and economic consequences, not a free shortcut.

## Central-bank tensions

An independent central bank can tighten policy while the executive seeks faster investment or employment growth. The conflict should be understandable: the institutions have different responsibilities and may disagree about economic conditions.

The player can explain fiscal plans, coordinate on information, adjust policy, and make requests. Public pressure may affect the political relationship but is not the same as lawful control over a rate decision. A mandate change or change in independence is a separate institutional reform.

Where monetary decisions are directly subject to executive control or shared internationally, use the researched arrangement instead. See [Interest rates & monetary choices]({{ '/interest-rates/' | relative_url }}) for how decisions reach households, firms, and the public budget.

## Leadership changes during the campaign

The preferred working model is **continuity of play across successive executives**. You control the current government; after a leadership change you take responsibility for the successor's agenda, within its mandate and coalition. You do not secretly control opposition actors before the transition.

An election, coalition breakdown, or other country-appropriate succession process can alter priorities, support, appointments, and available compromises. Do not insert competitive elections where the historical institutional structure does not support them.

The economic state persists: debt, infrastructure, contracts, enterprise ownership, court decisions, and unfinished projects do not reset. A successor can attempt to change policy, but needs the appropriate authority and must manage transition costs.

This proposal means losing office need not end the educational campaign. To preserve accountability, assess each administration's mandate separately and retain a whole-campaign record of promises, outcomes, and distributional effects. Exact election timing, succession triggers, and mandate selection remain open design choices.

## Country differences and scope

Research formal rules and effective institutional behavior separately. A constitutional power, a political convention, and the practical ability to use a power are not interchangeable. Record the evidence and uncertainty behind each profile.

Presidential, parliamentary, shared-power, and other governing arrangements should produce different routes to action. Judicial review and legislative autonomy must also vary as supported by evidence. None of these features should be inferred solely from public ownership or the size of government spending.

The first game needs a clear authority map, a few relevant legislative blocs, major legal disputes, and a succession rule. Detailed election campaigning, individual legislator management, and litigation procedure can remain outside the initial scope.

## Example and design checks

The [housing authorization example]({{ '/scenarios/' | relative_url }}#housing-legislation-courts-and-interest-rates) connects executive initiative, legislative amendment, judicial review, private lending, and an independent rate decision.

For every institutional encounter, ask: does the player understand who has authority, why the actor responded, what alternatives remain, and what happens next? If not, simplify the presentation or explain the rule rather than hiding it behind a generic approval score.
