## A separate dimension of policy

Trade openness and domestic market freedom are separate choices. The player can combine competitive domestic markets with protection, or substantial state ownership with extensive international trade.

Openness also has several dimensions: trade in goods, services, foreign direct investment, and financial flows. They should not all change together automatically. A country may welcome new factories while restricting some financial flows or strategic acquisitions.

The actions below are a proposed catalogue. Actual authority, legal conditions, and commitments depend on the nation and year.

## Action catalogue

| Action | What the player decides | What must be assessed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tariffs | Product coverage, rates, timing, and permitted exemptions | Import costs, domestic producers, users of imported inputs, revenue, and partner responses |
| Import quotas or licensing | Which goods are restricted and how access is allocated, where available | Availability, allocation privileges, substitution, administration, and potential evasion |
| Trade agreements | Priorities, concessions, standards, and negotiating partners | Counterparty acceptance, domestic authorization, implementation time, and adjustment costs |
| Industrial and export support | Eligible activities, funding, conditions, review dates, and expiry | Additional investment, public cost, dependency on support, and compatibility with commitments |
| Strategic export restrictions | Goods, destinations, licensing, and exceptions | Domestic exporters, foreign responses, supply-chain changes, and enforcement |
| Foreign investment policy | Entry conditions, ownership limits, screening, or facilitation | Financing, technology, competition, domestic control, and dependencies |
| Supplier diversification | Alternative partners, infrastructure, contracts, and reserve targets | Higher or lower costs, delivery times, reliability, and resilience |
| Sanctions and responses | Participation, scope, exceptions, and domestic adjustment measures where authorized | Exposure, enforcement, affected households and firms, and retaliation |

An unavailable measure should explain the missing authority or conflicting commitment. An institutional change or renegotiation may be possible, but it is a separate process with consequences.

## Constraints and shared authority

Tariffs and subsidies exist within international commitments. The [WTO's tariff overview](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/tariffs_e/tariffs_e.htm) explains tariff bindings, while its [overview of trade remedies and subsidies](https://www.wto.org/english/thewto_e/whatis_e/tif_e/agrm8_e.htm) describes conditions around particular measures. The game should represent relevant constraints without claiming every restriction or subsidy is either universally allowed or universally prohibited.

National control also varies. EU common commercial policy involves EU-level negotiation and decision-making. A German player therefore cannot be given unrestricted unilateral control over that shared policy. The [Council's description of the Trade Policy Committee](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/council-eu/search-the-list-of-council-preparatory-bodies/trade-policy-committee/) explains its role in assisting Commission negotiations on behalf of the EU. Each profile must identify national powers and the channels for influencing shared decisions.

An agreement is an outcome of negotiation. Choosing “seek an agreement” starts a process; it does not guarantee acceptance. A partner can request concessions, delay, reject, or accept. Legal commitments, exit procedures, and dispute responses need period-specific research.

## Foreign actors

The first design can represent a small number of important partner countries or blocs and broader world demand. It does not require every foreign household and firm to be individually simulated.

Each significant partner should have relevant industries, trade exposure, policy priorities, alternative suppliers, and bargaining constraints. Responses can include negotiation, formal dispute, retaliation, diversification, or continued trade. Retaliation should have a motive and costs to the responding partner; it is not an automatic reaction to every measure.

Foreign corporations respond separately from governments. They may accept lower margins, change suppliers, build local facilities, postpone investment, or exit a market. Their responses should respect available capacity and time.

## Trace costs through the economy

For a proposed tariff, show which goods are affected, who imports them, which domestic industries compete with them, and who uses them as inputs. A protection measure for one sector can raise costs for another.

The game should model how cost changes are shared among firms and customers under its stated assumptions. Do not promise that foreign producers always absorb the full cost, or that consumers always bear exactly the same amount. Alternative suppliers, competition, exchange rates, and contracts can change the result.

Adjustment takes time. A domestic producer needs equipment, workers, and financing before replacing imports. An exporter losing access to a market needs customers and logistics elsewhere. Announcing self-sufficiency cannot instantly create those resources.

## Exchange rates and financing

The nation's monetary arrangement determines what exchange-rate choices are available. A floating currency, a managed arrangement, and membership in a currency union should create different constraints. Detailed rules remain to be specified.

Changes in currency value should interact with import costs, export competitiveness, and debts denominated in other currencies. The report should identify which actors are exposed rather than present currency depreciation as a universal benefit.

Financial inflows, external borrowing, and access to foreign exchange connect trade to banking and public finances. Trade balance, government budget balance, and household welfare are separate outcomes; an improvement in one does not automatically mean the others improve.

## Resilience and industrial strategy

The player can decide which dependencies justify reserves, alternative suppliers, domestic production, or cooperation with partners. Each approach has maintenance, financing, and opportunity costs.

Support for a strategic industry should name the problem it addresses: missing infrastructure, training, financing, coordination, or a specific external benefit. Attach milestones and review dates so the player can distinguish learning and capacity growth from a permanent subsidy without progress.

The model should allow both successful development and failed investment. Success must depend on conditions and execution, not an automatic “strategic sector” bonus. A failed project can still leave useful skills or infrastructure, while a profitable project may fall short of its public promises.

## Immediate effects, delayed effects, and evaluation

Early effects may include changed import prices, orders, customs revenue, negotiations, and financing expectations. Later effects may include new capacity, relocated supply chains, productivity changes, retaliation, or dependence on continued support.

The annual report should show prices and availability of essentials, producer and downstream costs, employment changes, public costs, market access, and vulnerability to disruption. Trade volume alone is not a sufficient success metric.

## Open design choices

Decide how many partners to represent, whether negotiations use authored offers or adjustable terms, and how much dispute resolution is visible. Security issues should remain economic constraints and events initially; a military strategy layer is outside the proposed first scope.
