## Explain the consequence

The yearly report should answer three questions:

1. What changed?
2. Which decisions and conditions contributed?
3. What remains uncertain or still unfolding?

An explanation should connect actions with mechanisms: a project increased demand for construction workers; labor shortages delayed completion; completed housing expanded supply later. Avoid pretending that every outcome has one cause.

Offer a short explanation first, with optional detail about interacting systems, delayed effects, and affected groups.

## Separate facts, assumptions, and outcomes

The game needs three distinct kinds of information:

| Kind | Example | Presentation rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Historical starting fact | A nation's monetary institution at the scenario's start | Cite its source and date |
| Model assumption | How strongly credit conditions affect investment in this simulation | Explain the simplification and uncertainty |
| Simulated outcome | Employment changed after several interacting events | Identify it as a game result, not a historical fact or real-world forecast |

Debated mechanisms should not be smuggled in as established facts. Uncertainty should be meaningful and explainable rather than arbitrary randomness.

## Experiments and replay

Let the player revisit a decision under the same external shocks and compare outcomes. Hold external conditions consistent where possible, while allowing policy-dependent domestic events to diverge.

The comparison should show both the chosen mandate and side effects. A policy may meet its primary target while increasing costs or shifting risk elsewhere.

This teaches the behavior of the model. It does not prove that the same policy would have caused the same result in a real nation.

## Multiple perspectives

Reports should include how different groups experience a decision: a renter, a mortgage borrower, a small employer, a public-service worker, or a retired household. The exact groups should fit the nation and scenario.

Advisers could offer different interpretations of a dilemma. If included, they should disclose assumptions and uncertainties, and should not become caricatures of economic schools.

## Fair treatment of economic arrangements

Balanced design does not require every policy to be equally effective. It requires clear reasons for outcomes and consistent treatment of comparable mechanisms.

Public and private organizations can both innovate, misallocate resources, respond to incentives, or suffer from weak oversight. Markets and planning should encounter their respective information and coordination problems under conditions that make those problems relevant.

Historical calibration and sensitivity checks should help identify whether a supposedly neutral rule predetermines the ideological winner.

## Keep the game readable

Show a small dashboard and a few consequential choices. Deeper explanations should be available when curiosity or a surprising result motivates the player.

Mobile delivery supports this discipline: a yearly briefing, clear policy choices, and a readable outcome report. Detailed interface design and implementation remain outside this concept phase.
