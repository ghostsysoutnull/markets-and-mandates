## How to read these examples

These are **fictional design walkthroughs**, not historical reconstructions, forecasts, or calibrated simulation results. The years are relative turns. No numerical effect sizes have been established.

Each example follows one plausible branch. The alternatives show what should change when financing, implementation, competition, institutions, or policy choices differ. They are intended to test whether the proposed game offers understandable decisions.

Related measures are grouped into initiatives for these walkthroughs: for example, a housing-delivery package and a separate lending-rule reform. The exact initiative budget remains open. A package must still disclose the cost and administrative burden of every component; grouping policies must not permit unlimited free actions.

## Housing, credit, and bank distress

### Starting conditions

Housing costs are rising, construction is constrained, and two private banks have different strategies. A mortgage-heavy bank seeks more lending growth; a business-oriented bank is cautious about developers. Renters want more homes, existing owners value their assets, and construction firms need workers and materials.

The player wants better access to housing. The central bank is independent. The government has fiscal room for a limited program, but the construction sector cannot immediately expand without training and equipment.

### Turn 1: choose the intervention

The player starts public housing construction and offers targeted guarantees for qualifying development loans. They also propose tighter underwriting standards for highly leveraged household borrowing through the responsible institution.

The mortgage bank objects to the lending restriction and asks for a transition period. The business bank offers to finance qualifying construction but requests clearer guarantee terms. Developers bid for contracts; some ask for higher prices because capacity is scarce.

The report records committed spending, contingent guarantee exposure, planned homes, and the distinction between approved projects and completed homes. It does not report the full housing target as already delivered.

### Turn 2: capacity becomes the constraint

Construction demand rises before new homes are ready. In this branch, labor and material bottlenecks delay some projects. The mortgage bank shifts part of its new lending toward developers, creating a different concentration of risk.

The player can phase construction, support training, source more materials abroad, or revise contracts. Each has costs and delays. If broader inflation pressures also rise, the central bank may tighten policy; the explanation should not attribute the whole decision to housing alone.

The report distinguishes rent pressures, construction costs, loan rates, and employment. A rise in construction employment is not yet proof that housing became affordable.

### Turn 3: financing stress

A separate downturn weakens some borrowers. A bank reports losses on development loans and faces funding pressure. The player receives separate estimates of payment needs, loan losses, and public guarantee obligations.

The relevant authorities can consider liquidity support, restructuring, recapitalization, or resolution under the scenario's rules. The government may need to decide whether to commit public funds and on what terms. An emergency loan does not make bad assets sound; a recapitalization does not finish delayed housing.

The decision must identify whose claims are protected, who bears losses, and what happens to payment services and viable lending. Full shareholder protection should not be an inseparable part of protecting insured depositors.

### Turn 4: assess delivery and recovery

Some housing becomes available, completed projects expand capacity, and losses on failed projects are recognized. The report compares access for renters, household debt burdens, bank resilience, public cost, and remaining construction capacity.

An alternative run with better project screening, a slower construction schedule, or different lending concentration could avoid or reduce the banking stress. A weaker external shock could also change the outcome. The scenario must not force a crisis merely because the player used public housing or guarantees.

**Learning question:** Did the policy improve housing supply, inflate purchasing power without enough supply, or produce a mixture—and which constraints explain the result?

## Industrial support and trade retaliation

### Starting conditions

A large manufacturer proposes a new component factory. It wants subsidized finance, infrastructure, and protection from imports. Domestic exporters currently buy the same components abroad. An important trade partner supplies them and buys finished goods from the player's nation.

Assume this fictional nation has national authority over the relevant trade choices, subject to its commitments. A shared-policy nation would need a different decision route.

### Turn 1: negotiate a package

The player offers time-limited investment support tied to construction milestones, training, and reporting. They consider a targeted tariff only after checking the country's commitments and alternatives such as infrastructure or research support.

The corporation counters with a longer support period. Downstream exporters warn that more expensive components will hurt their competitiveness. The partner government seeks talks. These are competing interests the player can inspect, not a single business-opinion score.

### Turn 2: observe delivery and adaptation

In this branch, the package includes a permitted tariff change and the factory begins construction. Domestic buyers face higher component costs before new supply arrives. The corporation meets an initial milestone but requests extra funding because equipment costs increased.

The player can enforce the original terms, renegotiate with evidence, adjust support, or accept delay. Foreign suppliers may change prices or seek other customers. Customs revenue is reported separately from the subsidy cost and household effects.

### Turn 3: respond to the partner

The partner introduces a countermeasure within the scenario's assumed legal setting or pursues a dispute, depending on the case. Affected exporters seek help. The player can negotiate reciprocal concessions, modify the measure, diversify markets, or sustain the conflict while accepting costs.

The partner also faces domestic costs and political pressure. It should not retaliate endlessly without regard to its own interests.

### Turn 4: review the industrial objective

The factory may now produce components, but the report asks whether it can survive after support expires, whether quality and cost meet buyers' needs, and whether the promised training and employment occurred.

A successful branch delivers productive capacity and reduces a relevant dependency at an acceptable public cost. A failed branch leaves an uncompetitive operation demanding indefinite support. An alternative branch emphasizing infrastructure and market access could perform better or worse depending on the actual constraint.

**Learning question:** What additional capacity did the intervention create, who financed the transition, and what opportunities were lost elsewhere?

## Electricity reliability and ownership

### Starting conditions

Electricity is expensive and outages are frequent. A network operator needs investment; generators face fuel exposure; households need relief. The state has limited administrative capacity. The player wants dependable, affordable service.

This situation is compatible with different ownership patterns. The scenario briefing must identify which problem comes from generation, the grid, financing, fuel supply, or oversight.

### Turn 1: choose an institutional package

The player compares strengthening regulation under existing ownership, establishing public generation, purchasing the network, or a combination. Each option includes financing, service obligations, management, and a delivery schedule.

In this branch, the player purchases the network with compensation and sets a public reliability mandate. They retain private generation, procure additional capacity, and target temporary bill assistance to vulnerable households.

The existing grid, staff, debts, and maintenance backlog persist through the ownership change. A management transition can consume administrative capacity before it improves performance.

### Turn 2: manage conflicting objectives

The public operator requests funding for maintenance and replacement equipment. Ministers also want low bills. Keeping tariffs below the cost of the service without adequate compensation creates a financing problem in this branch.

The player can fund the obligation transparently, revise tariffs with targeted assistance, change the investment schedule, or reduce the promised service improvement. Independent reporting helps reveal whether missed targets come from funding, procurement, management, or physical constraints.

### Turn 3: evaluate contracts and enforcement

A private generator misses a contracted investment milestone. The regulator investigates using available staff and evidence. The firm attributes the delay to imported equipment and requests revised terms.

The player must distinguish a genuine disruption from a poorly planned or opportunistic request. Depending on contract and authority, remedies can include adjusted deadlines, penalties, replacement procurement, or changes to future contracts.

### Turn 4: compare outcomes

Completed maintenance and capacity can improve reliability, while fuel exposure or weak execution may leave problems unresolved. The report separates outages, access, household bills, fiscal transfers, environmental effects, and outstanding obligations.

A comparison run under private ownership with credible regulation and financing may also improve reliability. A poorly governed public or private operator may fail. The explanation must identify mechanisms rather than attribute the result solely to the ownership label.

**Learning question:** Which combination of ownership, incentives, financing, information, and oversight delivered the service?

## Using scenarios to refine the spec

For each walkthrough, review whether the player has an understandable objective, meaningful alternatives, adequate information, and a reason to care about the next turn. If a branch always wins regardless of starting conditions, examine the assumptions before treating it as a lesson.

Later quantitative scenarios should state initial values, uncertainty, accounting relationships, and the rules producing each outcome. These examples intentionally stop at conceptual behavior; they do not establish those numerical rules.
