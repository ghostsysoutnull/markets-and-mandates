## A core player tool

Domestic regulation belongs among the player's main actions. It determines what organizations may do, their obligations, the protections people receive, and the remedies available when rules are broken.

Regulation should have several dimensions. Competition enforcement, worker protection, financial resilience, utility-service obligations, and barriers to entry are different choices. “More regulation” should not be a single slider with a fixed growth penalty or welfare bonus.

All actions below are proposed game tools. Their legal availability and institutional owner must be checked for the nation and starting year.

## Action catalogue

| Area | Player proposals | Responses the model should consider |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Competition | Investigate abuse, review mergers, require access to essential networks, seek structural remedies through the relevant process | Entry, price and quality changes, legal challenges, compliance costs, and changes to investment |
| Banking | Change capital or liquidity rules through the responsible institution, restrict risky lending, strengthen supervision and consumer protection | Portfolio changes, repricing, retained earnings, new capital, reduced risk, or avoidance attempts |
| Labor | Change wage floors, safety standards, dismissal rules, bargaining arrangements, and training support | Wage bargaining, hiring, productivity investment, prices, formalization, and compliance |
| Utilities | Set service obligations, access rules, pricing arrangements, and investment requirements | Maintenance, service coverage, financing needs, quality, and entry where feasible |
| Consumers and products | Set safety, disclosure, warranty, and lending-conduct standards | Trust, product changes, compliance burdens, and enforcement disputes |
| Business entry | Simplify permits, revise licenses, improve registration, or impose justified qualifications | New entry, administrative workload, safety outcomes, and incumbent responses |
| Investment and ownership | Screen strategic acquisitions or change foreign-ownership limits | Financing, new entrants, technology access, uncertainty, and security-related dependencies |

These response lists are possibilities to assess, not guaranteed effects. Magnitude and direction depend on the rule, starting conditions, implementation, and actor behavior. The [OECD's competition assessment work](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/sub-issues/competitive-and-fair-markets/competition-assessment.html) is an initial reference for examining how rules affect entry and rivalry.

Carbon-emissions and climate-change regulation is outside the accepted game scope: no carbon taxes, emissions trading, decarbonisation targets or related compliance challenges. Other environmental regulation, such as local pollution controls, is not selected by this catalogue and would need its own scope decision.

## Designing a rule

A regulation proposal should state its objective and target problem, the actors covered, its standard or restriction, and which institution administers it. It also needs an implementation schedule, funding, penalties or remedies, review arrangements, and expected uncertainties.

For example, a new industrial workplace-safety standard might cover large plants first, give existing facilities a transition period, fund inspectors, and offer conditional support for equipment replacement. The player should see why those choices change costs, speed, coverage, and worker exposure to hazards.

Exemptions are a policy decision. They may accommodate genuine constraints or create loopholes and unequal treatment. Actors should be able to argue for them; the player should see who benefits.

## Passing a law and enforcing it

Use a visible sequence: **proposal → legal authorization → preparation → enforcement → review**. Some stages may overlap; a rule should not become fully effective merely because the player selects it.

The executive can act under existing delegated authority or seek legislation, depending on the measure. Lawmakers may amend or reject a proposal. Courts may review a measure through the applicable legal process; legislative approval does not automatically settle every legal objection. The [government chapter]({{ '/government/' | relative_url }}) specifies how these tensions become player decisions.

Institutions need staff, expertise, data, inspection capacity, and procedures. Where relevant, regional governments, courts, or independent regulators hold part of the authority. Funding a department does not automatically give it powers it lacks.

Limited capacity forces choices about investigation priorities. Understaffed enforcement can leave violations undetected or cases unresolved. Increasing penalties without improving detection should not automatically deliver universal compliance.

Simplifying unnecessary procedures can also improve enforcement: a clearer rule may be easier for small firms to follow and for inspectors to assess.

## NPC responses and political pressure

Corporations and banks can comply, adapt products, invest in cleaner or safer processes, adjust prices, seek exemptions, lobby, or challenge a decision. Some may attempt concealment when incentives and oversight make it plausible.

Distinguish lobbying, legal challenge, and corruption. A petition is not automatically corruption; a court challenge may reveal a genuine legal limit. Capture is a proposed failure mechanism when oversight systematically serves regulated actors at the expense of its mandate.

Workers, consumers, competing businesses, and local communities can support or oppose a measure for different reasons. Reports should state those reasons instead of describing all resistance as ideological.

## Immediate and delayed effects

Early effects may include compliance spending, inspection workload, uncertainty during rulemaking, and changes to announced investments. Later effects may include lower harm, new entry, improved resilience, innovation, or greater concentration if smaller firms cannot meet fixed compliance costs.

These mechanisms can pull in different directions. Pairing a rule with technical assistance, transitional funding, or simpler reporting may change who can comply. Deregulation may remove a needless barrier or remove a useful protection; the report must identify which mechanism is operating.

## Banking regulation is institution-specific

Capital requirements, liquidity requirements, underwriting restrictions, deposit protection, supervision, and resolution should remain distinct concepts. The [Basel Framework](https://www.bis.org/committees/bcbs/basel-framework) supplies international prudential standards; national application and historical timing require separate research.

An announced tightening could allow a phase-in. Banks may raise capital, retain earnings, alter their portfolios, or reduce new lending. The model should not assume that a change has only one possible response.

Supervisors and central banks may have independent powers. A government proposal should identify whether the player can enact it, request action, or must first change the institutional framework. Financial stability responsibilities need not all belong to the central bank.

## Evaluation and failure modes

For each major rule, show the intended outcome, observed compliance, enforcement costs, unintended burdens, distributional effects, and remaining uncertainty. Where the effect takes time, indicate that the evaluation is incomplete.

Possible failures include a rule that targets the wrong problem, a loophole, weak enforcement, selective treatment, an unrealistic deadline, or repeal before firms finish adapting. A rule can also succeed at its primary goal while imposing substantial costs that deserve discussion.

Use review dates to let the player adjust scope, strengthen implementation, simplify a requirement, or repeal it. Reversal can require its own transition; existing investments and contracts do not disappear.
