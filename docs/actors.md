## Role in the game

Large corporations and private banks are **non-player characters (NPCs)**. They respond to the economy and your policies, make proposals, and sometimes create dilemmas. Their actions should have identifiable motives and economic consequences.

The working proposal is a small, visible cast of strategically important organizations, with smaller firms and households represented in groups. This avoids making the entire economy depend on a handful of named companies while giving the player actors they can recognize across turns.

Use fictional organizations grounded in researched sector conditions initially. Whether to use real company names is unresolved; invented misconduct or political behavior should never be presented as historical fact about a real organization.

## What an actor knows and wants

Each important actor should have a short profile: sector, ownership, employment, productive capacity, financial condition, dependencies, objectives, and current plans. The player sees public information and regulator findings; internal intentions remain estimates unless disclosed.

Actors evaluate opportunities using imperfect information. They can make mistakes, revise plans, or disagree about the future. They cannot foresee the next random shock or inspect a hidden future outcome.

An actor's response should explain its relevant incentive: “We postponed the factory because borrowing costs rose and orders fell.” Avoid unexplained hostility or an arbitrary approval meter that decides all behavior.

## Large corporations

### Objectives and differences

Private corporations seek financial survival and returns, but can pursue different strategies: expansion, stable dividends, innovation, market share, or control of a critical supply chain. Management priorities and existing commitments shape the response.

| Proposed actor type | What makes its decisions distinct |
| --- | --- |
| Domestic manufacturer | Relies on local workers, industrial infrastructure, financing, and material inputs |
| Multinational exporter | Compares investment locations, exchange-rate exposure, transport costs, and market access |
| Energy or network operator | Has expensive fixed infrastructure, service obligations, and limited short-term flexibility |
| Technology or platform company | Invests in innovation and may benefit from network effects; faces entry, interoperability, and competition questions |
| Major retailer or distributor | Connects imports, supplier terms, logistics, wages, and consumer demand |
| Property developer | Depends on land, permits, construction capacity, household purchasing power, and credit |

These are design archetypes, not mandatory stereotypes. The final cast should reflect the nation and chosen year.

### Decisions corporations make

Corporations can expand or postpone investment; hire, train, bargain over wages, or reduce employment; change output, prices, quality, and suppliers; seek financing; propose mergers; and enter or leave markets. Their practical options depend on resources, contracts, law, and demand.

They may lobby, challenge a decision in court, request subsidies, or propose a public contract. An actor may evade a rule when incentives and weak oversight permit, with a risk of investigation and consequences. Evasion is neither universal nor cost-free.

Relocation should have a credible timescale. A firm can redirect a future factory more easily than move an existing power grid. Exit threats should be assessed against assets, alternative locations, contracts, and switching costs.

### Player influence

The player changes taxes, regulations, competition policy, infrastructure, procurement, trade access, and available public support. They can negotiate an investment package where institutional powers allow it. Nationalization is a separate ownership reform with financing and management requirements.

A subsidy offer can include investment milestones, wage or training commitments, reporting, an expiry date, and recovery of funds if terms are broken. The NPC can accept, counteroffer, or decline. The government must then monitor delivery.

### Consequences and failure modes

Corporate growth can increase output, employment, innovation, and tax revenue. It can also create concentrated dependencies or market power. A large firm is not automatically a monopoly; the model needs to distinguish size, efficiency, and barriers facing competitors.

Repeated rescue or unconditional support may change an actor's expectations. Conversely, abrupt withdrawal of a critical supplier can disrupt other sectors. Reports should identify these exposures rather than award a generic “business confidence” bonus for every concession.

## Private banks

### Objectives and constraints

Private banks seek earnings and survival while managing loan losses, funding, withdrawals, and regulatory obligations. Proposed profiles include a mortgage-heavy retail bank, a business lender, and a bank dependent on more volatile funding. A nation's actual mix requires research.

Keep three concepts distinguishable: **capital** absorbs losses, **liquidity** supports payments when due, and **credit quality** concerns whether borrowers repay. A bank can face a payment shortage without all its loans being worthless. Emergency liquidity alone does not erase underlying losses. See the [Bank of England's explanation of bank capital and liquidity](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/2013/q3/bank-capital-and-liquidity).

### Decisions banks make

- Select borrowers and sectors, including households, small firms, developers, and large enterprises.
- Set loan rates, fees, collateral requirements, and lending standards within the rules.
- Compete for deposits and other funding; retain earnings, distribute profits, or seek new capital.
- Restructure troubled loans, reduce exposures, or tighten new lending.
- Request central-bank liquidity or government assistance when eligible and under pressure.

The central-bank policy rate influences conditions but does not force every bank to offer the same rate. Funding costs, expected losses, competition, and balance-sheet strength should also matter.

### Player influence and institutional limits

The player can propose financial legislation, fund supervision, establish a development bank, offer targeted guarantees, or arrange a recapitalization where legally available. Independent supervisors and central banks exercise their own powers. The player cannot directly order every private loan unless a legally available directed-credit arrangement is introduced.

Guarantees create public exposure if loans fail. A rescue package should specify who contributes and who bears losses: shareholders, relevant creditors, industry funds, or the public, subject to the scenario's legal protections. Depositor protection is a distinct choice from protecting bank owners.

### Consequences and failure modes

Credit can enable productive investment or finance a property boom. A bank concentrating on one sector becomes exposed to that sector's problems. Tightening lending can preserve an individual bank's position while weakening borrowers and employment elsewhere.

The game should show that private lending decisions interact with the public rules and with loan demand. A low policy rate does not guarantee a willing, creditworthy borrower or a bank able to expand lending.

## Public enterprises, development banks, and cooperatives

Use the same basic organizational needs—workers, inputs, financing, management, information—across ownership forms. Change the mandate and accountability arrangements.

A public enterprise may prioritize universal service, strategic capacity, employment, and financial sustainability. Those objectives can conflict. The player can set a service obligation and compensation, appoint management under the relevant rules, and choose how results are assessed.

A development bank can support projects underserved by other lenders, but needs project assessment, funding, and a process for handling losses. A cooperative can prioritize member benefits while still facing financing and market constraints.

Changing ownership should preserve the enterprise's physical assets, contracts, debts, and workforce unless the reform explicitly changes them. A new owner does not instantly remove a shortage or supply competent managers.

## Other responding actors

| Actor | Meaningful behavior |
| --- | --- |
| Households | Adjust consumption, saving, borrowing, work, and location within their means |
| Small firms and new entrants | Enter markets, adopt technology, hire, or close as opportunities and constraints change |
| Unions and worker groups | Bargain, support reforms, negotiate transition terms, or organize industrial action |
| Legislative blocs | Support, amend, delay, or oppose legislation according to constituencies, priorities, and institutional rules |
| Regulators | Investigate and enforce rules within their mandates, powers, and capacity |
| Courts | Review authority, process, rights, and obligations through the legal procedures and remedies available |
| Central bank | Respond to conditions under its mandate and monetary arrangement |
| Foreign governments and trade blocs | Negotiate, retaliate, diversify suppliers, or cooperate according to their own interests |

## Encounters and deals

A proposed encounter presents the actor's request, its stated reasons, the evidence available, the terms on offer, and the player's options. The player may request information, negotiate conditions, accept, decline, or change the broader policy instead.

Deals should persist across turns. Track promised capacity, disbursed funds, deadlines, compliance findings, and unresolved disputes. The player needs to see the difference between an announcement and a completed investment.

Not every actor needs a meeting every year. Surface encounters when an important decision, conflict, or dependency warrants attention. The player governs institutions and strategic choices, not every firm's daily operations.
